# Daily Sleep Tracking

## Real-world use case

A watch reports last night’s duration, bedtime, and sometimes sleep stages. The user wants to know if they “got enough sleep.” Duration is only one part of sleep health. Feeling restored, regular timing, and daytime function matter as much as a single night’s hours.

## Guidance from health agencies and clinics

CDC states that adults generally need 7 or more hours of sleep and that many adults sleep less than that. NHLBI summarizes expert advice that adults sleep 7 to 9 hours, notes that regularly sleeping under 7 hours is associated with more health problems, and says sleeping more than 9 hours is not always harmful (for example during recovery from illness or sleep loss). Children’s needs are higher and age-specific.

Harvard Health’s sleep blog emphasizes quality and how a person feels the next day, not only the clock. One or two poor nights can follow stress, alcohol, a late meal, or travel. Persistent trouble falling or staying asleep, or unrefreshing sleep despite enough time in bed, is a reason for clinical review rather than chasing a wearable stage percentage. Harvard Health sleep-hygiene writing also covers a consistent schedule, a dark quiet room, and daytime habits.

## Wearable limits

Movement and heart-rate algorithms can mark quiet wakefulness as sleep and can miss nights when the watch is on a charger. Stage breakdowns are estimates and do not replace a sleep study. A long recorded duration with frequent awakenings is not automatically healthy sleep.

## Safe interpretation

Report recorded duration and, if present, time in bed. Do not diagnose insomnia or sleep apnea from one night. Loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, and dangerous sleepiness while driving need clinical assessment. Do not recommend a sleeping pill.
