# Hypertension and Sleep

## Purpose and relationship

Sleep duration, quality, and regularity are associated with cardiovascular health. Persistent sleep problems may coexist with hypertension, but a sleep pattern does not diagnose high blood pressure or establish its cause.

## Key facts and wearable data

Adults generally need seven or more hours of sleep, with individual variation. Shift work, stress, environment, illness, caregiving, medicines, and sleep disorders affect sleep. Obstructive sleep apnea is clinically relevant to hypertension but cannot be diagnosed from snoring alone or a consumer sleep score.

Wearables estimate sleep and may help identify repeated changes. They can misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep and usually do not provide clinical-grade sleep staging. A short night may temporarily affect wellbeing; it is not by itself a chronic sleep disorder or proof of elevated pressure.

## Safe interpretation

Report duration, regularity, device type, comparison period, symptoms, and missing data. Do not infer blood pressure from sleep, heart rate, or steps. Do not claim causation from a time-aligned trend. Missing sleep data may indicate non-wear. Never recommend altering medicine based on sleep-device data.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine advice for persistent insomnia symptoms, excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring or witnessed breathing pauses, or repeated blood-pressure concerns. Individual assessment may be needed when sleep and cardiovascular conditions coexist.
