# Self-Checked Blood Glucose

## Real-world use case

Someone living with diabetes, or someone asked to check glucose at home, records a finger-stick or sensor reading next to meals, activity, or illness. A daily summary may also show steps or sleep from a watch. The glucose value is the clinical signal; the wearable fields are only possible context.

## What diabetes organizations say

The American Diabetes Association describes checking blood sugar as a way to understand patterns and support a known care plan. Timing (fasting, before meals, after meals, bedtime, or around exercise) changes meaning. ADA diagnosis pages describe laboratory criteria such as fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and oral glucose-tolerance testing. Those tests are not the same as one consumer meter reading.

NHS hypoglycaemia guidance explains that low blood sugar can cause sweating, shaking, confusion, and, if severe, collapse. People who use insulin or some other glucose-lowering medicines need an individualized hypo plan from their care team. This knowledge base must not invent a treatment dose.

## Wearable relationship

Consumer step, calorie, and heart-rate totals cannot replace a glucose meter or prescribed continuous glucose monitor. Activity and sleep may help explain why a recorded glucose moved; they cannot invent a missing glucose value or prove that diabetes is present or absent.

## Safe interpretation

- Report the recorded glucose with unit, time, and meal or activity context if those were provided.
- Do not diagnose diabetes from one high reading or “rule out” diabetes from one typical reading.
- Do not infer medicine adherence.
- Severe confusion, seizure, or inability to swallow in a person at risk of low glucose is an emergency.
