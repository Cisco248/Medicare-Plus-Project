# Body Mass Index and Recorded Weight

## Real-world use case

A profile stores height and weight, and a daily or weekly view shows BMI. Users treat the category label as a personal diagnosis. CDC states that BMI is a screening measure of weight relative to height, not a diagnosis of disease.

## Adult categories used in screening

For adults 20 and older, CDC groups BMI as underweight (below 18.5), healthy weight (18.5 to 24.9), overweight (25.0 to 29.9), and obesity (30.0 or greater), with further obesity classes at 30, 35, and 40. WHO discusses overweight and obesity as population health issues associated with higher risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and some cancers. These categories do not replace clinical assessment.

## Limits of BMI

BMI does not measure body fat, fat location, muscle, bone, or fitness. Muscular athletes can land in overweight or obesity categories without excess fat. Age, sex, and ancestry can change how BMI relates to risk. Mayo Clinic education notes that BMI should be read with lifestyle, waist size, blood pressure, lipids, and other findings. A calculated BMI from self-entered height and weight inherits any measurement error.

## Safe interpretation

- Compute or report BMI only when both height and weight are recorded. If either is missing, BMI is unknown.
- Use the CDC category as a screening label, not as a diagnosis of obesity disease or “healthy proof.”
- Do not invent an ideal weight. Do not recommend a crash diet, supplement, or medicine.
- Rapid unintended weight change is a clinical concern independent of the BMI category.
