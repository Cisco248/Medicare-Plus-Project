# How Diabetes Is Identified

Also asked as: how the identify the diabetes, how do I know if I have diabetes, how is diabetes diagnosed, what tests detect diabetes.

## Question: How is diabetes diagnosed?

### Answer

Diabetes is diagnosed by a qualified clinician using validated laboratory glucose or HbA1c tests, symptoms when relevant, and repeat testing when guidelines require confirmation. It is not diagnosed by a wearable, a step count, or a machine-learning e-doc prediction.

### Important safety information

Do not treat a MediCare Plus “Diabetic / High Risk” label as a diagnosis. Do not ignore symptoms because a prediction said low risk.

## Question: How can I identify diabetes?

### Answer

Possible clues include symptoms such as thirst, passing urine more often, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing sores, and recurrent infections, plus risk factors such as family history, higher BMI, and age. Identification still requires blood tests interpreted by a clinician. Many people with type 2 diabetes have few early symptoms.

### Explanation

CDC and WHO describe common symptoms, but symptoms have many other causes. Feeling well does not rule out diabetes. One high home glucose reading suggests the need for clinical review; it is not a stand-alone diagnosis.

## Diagnostic laboratory concepts

The American Diabetes Association publishes educational diagnostic cut-points for laboratory tests. These are diagnostic thresholds, not emergency thresholds and not home-meter rules.

- Fasting plasma glucose at or above 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L).
- 2-hour plasma glucose during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test at or above 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L).
- HbA1c at or above 6.5% (48 mmol/mol).
- Random plasma glucose at or above 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in a person with classic hyperglycaemia symptoms.

WHO diabetes fact sheets use the same core plasma-glucose diagnostic concepts. Confirmation rules, pregnancy criteria, and anaemia or haemoglobin-variant limits for HbA1c must follow the clinician and local laboratory.

## Reference range versus diagnostic threshold

A laboratory reference range is the interval a lab uses for a typical population. A diagnostic threshold is the value used to classify diabetes or prediabetes. They are not always identical. An e-doc glucose field is a submitted number in mmol/L; it is not automatically a fasting plasma glucose diagnostic test.

## Home glucose versus laboratory diagnosis

Finger-stick meters and many sensors measure capillary or interstitial glucose. Diagnostic criteria above refer to laboratory plasma glucose or standardized HbA1c. Timing (fasting versus after eating) changes meaning. The diabetes form hint is “mmol/L if known.” A value such as 180 is more typical of mg/dL than mmol/L; unit confusion should be checked with a clinician rather than assumed.

## Prediabetes laboratory concepts

ADA educational prediabetes ranges include HbA1c 5.7–6.4%, fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L), or 2-hour OGTT 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11.0 mmol/L). These do not mean the person “already has diabetes.”

## What the e-doc does not do

The diabetes e-doc collects age, gender, pulse rate, blood pressure, glucose, BMI, family diabetes, and hypertensive status, then runs a scaled classifier. Output labels are “Diabetic / High Risk” or “Non-Diabetic / Low Risk.” That output is a model risk class plus a probability confidence score. It is not a WHO or ADA diagnostic statement.

## When to seek tests

Seek clinical advice for persistent symptoms, gestational-diabetes history, strong family history, or repeated unexpected glucose values. Emergency symptoms (collapse, seizure, severe confusion, inability to swallow, severe breathing difficulty) need emergency services, not an e-doc form.
