# Diabetes and Diet

## Purpose and relationship

Food and drink affect glucose, cardiovascular risk, and overall nutrition. There is no single diet that is correct for every person with diabetes. A sustainable eating pattern should reflect culture, access, preferences, health conditions, and individualized clinical goals.

## Key facts

Authoritative guidance generally supports vegetables, fruits, whole grains and other high-fiber foods, legumes, and appropriate protein sources while limiting free sugars, excess sodium, and heavily processed foods. Carbohydrate amount and timing can affect glucose, but food should not be labeled simply “good” or “bad.” Sugary drinks can contribute substantial free sugar with limited nutritional value.

## Data and safe interpretation

Meal logs and wearable calorie estimates are often incomplete. Calories burned by a device do not show glucose response, nutritional quality, or how much someone should eat. A single meal or glucose reading does not establish the quality of a person’s usual diet. Missing food data mean intake is unknown.

RAG output should describe recorded patterns and uncertainty, not prescribe a restrictive diet. It must not recommend changing insulin or any other medicine based on food records. Individual carbohydrate planning, kidney disease, pregnancy, eating disorders, allergies, and risk of low glucose require professional input.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine advice from a qualified clinician or dietitian for individualized meal planning, recurring unexpected glucose patterns, difficulty obtaining adequate nutrition, or questions about food and medicines. Rapid unexplained weight change or persistent symptoms also warrants assessment.
