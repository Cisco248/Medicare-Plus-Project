# Diabetes Medications (Educational)

## Question: What medications are used for diabetes?

### Answer

Medicines depend on diabetes type, other conditions, and clinician judgment. Type 1 diabetes requires insulin. Type 2 diabetes may be treated with lifestyle measures alone at first or with tablets and/or insulin. Gestational diabetes medicines, if used, are pregnancy-specific.

### Important safety information

This is class-level education only. It is not a prescription, dose, or instruction to start, stop, or switch a medicine. Never stop a prescribed diabetes medicine because of a chatbot or e-doc result.

## Insulin

Insulin replaces or supplements the hormone the pancreas makes. It lowers blood glucose. The main educational risk is hypoglycaemia. Timing with food and activity is individualized. The diabetes e-doc form does not collect insulin dose.

## Metformin

Metformin is a commonly used first-line medicine in type 2 diabetes in many guidelines, including NICE NG28 educational summaries. It helps the body use insulin more effectively and can reduce glucose production by the liver. It is not insulin. Gastrointestinal effects can occur. Kidney function and other conditions affect whether it is suitable; that decision is clinical.

## Other glucose-lowering classes

Other classes (discussed in ADA and NICE educational materials) include medicines that increase insulin release, increase insulin sensitivity, slow carbohydrate absorption, increase urinary glucose excretion, or mimic gut hormones. Names and suitability vary. Some classes have extra heart or kidney evidence in selected people; a clinician decides. This knowledge base does not rank brands.

## Medicines that can cause low glucose

Insulin and some tablet classes can cause hypoglycaemia. People using them need a clinician-provided hypo plan. Sweating, shaking, confusion, or collapse needs the person’s plan or emergency care.

## Adherence

Taking medicine as prescribed, with a refill plan, supports glucose management. Missing doses can raise glucose; extra doses can be dangerous. Do not double a dose after a miss unless the care team already gave that instruction.

## Relationship to the model

The diabetes prediction model does not include a medication feature. A “Non-Diabetic / Low Risk” result does not mean medicines should stop. A “Diabetic / High Risk” result does not mean a specific drug should start.
