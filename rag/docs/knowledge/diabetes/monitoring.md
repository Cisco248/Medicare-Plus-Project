# Diabetes Monitoring

## Purpose

Monitoring helps a person and clinical team understand glucose patterns, manage known diabetes, and screen for complications. Monitoring is not identical to diagnosis, and individual schedules and targets depend on diabetes type, treatment, pregnancy, health status, and clinical guidance.

## Clinical information

Relevant information can include capillary or continuous glucose readings, HbA1c, blood pressure, lipid profile, kidney tests, eye screening, and foot assessment. Preserve the test or device name, value, unit, timestamp, meal or activity context when available, and whether a reading was clinically measured, self-tested, or device-estimated.

HbA1c reflects average glucose exposure over a longer period; it is not interchangeable with a current glucose reading. Continuous glucose sensors measure interstitial glucose and can differ from blood glucose, particularly during rapid change. A single unexpected result should be interpreted under the person’s established care instructions and may require confirmation.

## Wearable relationship

Steps, activity, sleep, heart rate, and calories can help explain context but cannot validate glucose, diagnose a complication, or prove treatment success. Time alignment may reveal an association without proving cause.

## Safe interpretation

- Diagnosis and targets require qualified clinical interpretation.
- Distinguish repeated trends from isolated values and sensor artifacts.
- Represent missing data as “not available,” never “normal.”
- Do not infer medicine adherence or recommend starting, stopping, or changing medicine.
- Escalate severe symptoms according to `warning_signs.md`, not according to an activity score.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine advice for recurring unexpected readings, uncertainty about device accuracy, monitoring frequency, individual targets, or overdue complication checks.
