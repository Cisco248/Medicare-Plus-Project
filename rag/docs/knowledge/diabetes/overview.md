# Diabetes Overview

Also called: diabetes mellitus, high blood sugar, high glucose, sugar disease.

## Question: What is diabetes?

### Answer

Diabetes is a group of chronic conditions in which blood glucose (blood sugar) stays too high because the body does not make enough insulin, cannot use insulin effectively, or both.

### Explanation

Glucose is the main sugar in blood and a major energy source for the body. Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas that helps glucose move from blood into cells. When insulin is missing, too low, or not working well, glucose remains in the blood. Persistently high glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves and affect the heart, kidneys, eyes, and feet.

### Related concepts

Diabetes mellitus, type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, gestational diabetes, prediabetes, insulin, insulin resistance, blood glucose.

### Important safety information

This is general education. It does not diagnose diabetes and does not replace evaluation by a qualified healthcare professional. A MediCare Plus prediction is not a clinical diagnosis.

## Question: What happens when someone has diabetes?

### Answer

When someone has diabetes, blood glucose is higher than the body can keep in a healthy range without treatment and support. The person may have no obvious symptoms, especially early in type 2 diabetes, or may notice thirst, urination, fatigue, blurred vision, or infections.

### Explanation

High glucose over time is associated with cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, eye disease, nerve damage, and foot problems. Acute emergencies can include severe low glucose (hypoglycaemia) and severe high glucose states. Feeling well does not prove glucose is in a safe range.

## Question: What is high blood sugar?

### Answer

High blood sugar means blood glucose is above the range expected for the situation. People also call this hyperglycaemia or “sugar is high.”

### Explanation

Meaning depends on the test, unit, timing (fasting, after meals, random), and whether the person already has diabetes. A single home reading is not the same as a laboratory diagnostic test. Very high glucose with vomiting, confusion, or breathing difficulty needs urgent care.

## Question: Why is my sugar high?

### Answer

Many factors can raise glucose, including meals (especially sugary drinks and large carbohydrate portions), illness, stress, inactivity, some medicines, missed diabetes treatment, and insulin deficiency or insulin resistance.

### Explanation

The chatbot cannot name the exact cause for one person from a single number. A submitted e-doc glucose value is one input the diabetes model uses; it does not by itself prove a diagnosis or explain why that reading occurred.

## Key facts

- Type 1 diabetes is not caused by low physical activity.
- Type 2 diabetes is associated with multiple genetic, social, metabolic, and behavioral factors.
- Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy and needs pregnancy-specific care.
- Prediabetes means glucose is higher than typical but does not meet diabetes diagnostic criteria; it is a risk state, not a guarantee of future diabetes.

## Activity and wearable context

Steps, active minutes, heart rate, calories, and sleep estimates can describe behavior and may support conversations about self-management. They do not measure blood glucose and cannot establish diabetes, diabetes type, glucose control, or complications.

## Safe interpretation

- A documented diagnosis is different from a risk factor, a symptom, or a machine-learning prediction.
- Laboratory results should retain test name, value, unit, date, and clinical context.
- One glucose result may be affected by timing, meals, illness, and testing conditions.
- Missing glucose or HbA1c data are unknown, not normal.
- Never infer medicine effectiveness or recommend starting, stopping, or changing medicine from these materials.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine clinical advice for possible diabetes symptoms, concern about risk, repeated unexpected glucose readings, or questions about monitoring and individual targets.
