# Diabetes Overview

## Definition and purpose

Diabetes is a group of chronic conditions in which blood glucose is elevated because the body does not produce enough insulin, cannot use insulin effectively, or both. Major forms include type 1, type 2, and gestational diabetes. This document supplies general context; diagnosis requires appropriate clinical assessment and validated testing.

## Key facts

Persistently elevated glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves and affect the heart, kidneys, eyes, and feet. Type 1 diabetes is not caused by low activity. Type 2 diabetes is associated with multiple genetic, social, metabolic, and behavioral factors. Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy and needs pregnancy-specific care.

## Activity and wearable context

Steps, active minutes, heart rate, calories, and sleep estimates can describe behavior and may support conversations about self-management. They do not measure blood glucose and cannot establish diabetes, diabetes type, glucose control, or complications. A change from a personal activity baseline is a trend, not a diagnosis.

## Safe interpretation

- A documented diagnosis is different from a risk factor or symptom.
- Laboratory results should retain test name, value, unit, date, and clinical context.
- One glucose result may be affected by timing, meals, illness, and testing conditions; do not label a persistent condition from one value.
- Missing glucose or HbA1c data are unknown, not normal.
- Never infer medicine effectiveness or recommend starting, stopping, or changing medicine from these materials.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine clinical advice for possible diabetes symptoms, concern about risk, repeated unexpected glucose readings, or questions about monitoring and individual targets. People with known diabetes should use the follow-up plan agreed with their care team.
