# Diabetes and Sleep

## Purpose and relationship

Sleep supports physical and mental health. Sleep duration, timing, and quality are associated with metabolic health and diabetes self-management, but sleep alone neither causes nor diagnoses diabetes.

## Key facts and wearable data

Adults generally need at least seven hours of sleep, although needs vary. Illness, stress, work schedules, caregiving, environment, sleep disorders, glucose symptoms, and medicines can affect sleep. Consumer wearables estimate sleep from movement and physiological signals; they do not directly measure sleep stages with the accuracy of a clinical sleep study.

Repeated short or disrupted sleep may provide useful context alongside symptoms and clinical measurements. One short night is not a chronic pattern. Long time in bed is not necessarily restorative sleep, and missing device data are not evidence of being awake.

## Safe interpretation

Do not infer glucose level, insulin effectiveness, diabetes control, sleep apnea, or insomnia from sleep duration, resting heart rate, or next-day steps. Keep association separate from cause. Describe the recorded period, device limitations, personal baseline, and missing data. Do not recommend changing medicine or glucose-management plans because of wearable sleep data.

## Routine clinical advice

Seek routine clinical advice for persistent difficulty sleeping, excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring or witnessed breathing pauses, or recurring nighttime glucose concerns. Individual advice is especially important when sleep disruption may interact with diabetes treatment or safety-sensitive work.
