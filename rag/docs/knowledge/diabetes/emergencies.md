# Diabetes Emergencies

## Question: What should I do during a diabetes emergency?

### Answer

Contact local emergency services immediately for loss of consciousness, seizure, severe confusion, inability to swallow safely, severe breathing difficulty, or rapidly worsening severe illness. Do not give food or drink to an unconscious person. Do not wait for another e-doc prediction or wearable reading.

### Important safety information

Emergency care comes first. Chatbot education must not delay help. There is no single universal glucose number in this knowledge base that by itself defines every emergency, because device, unit, treatment, and symptoms all matter.

## Question: What is severe hypoglycaemia?

### Answer

Severe hypoglycaemia is very low blood glucose that the person cannot treat themselves. ADA education uses an alert value below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) for many people who check glucose, with lower values more dangerous. Symptoms can include sweating, shaking, hunger, confusion, seizure, or collapse. People who use insulin or some other glucose-lowering medicines need an individualized hypo plan from their care team. This knowledge base must not invent a treatment dose.

## Question: What is severe hyperglycaemia?

### Answer

Severe high glucose with vomiting, drowsiness, rapid breathing, dehydration, or confusion can be a medical emergency, including diabetic ketoacidosis or a hyperosmolar state. ADA hyperglycemia education explains that very high glucose needs a sick-day or emergency plan. Do not assume a home meter value alone tells the full story.

## Question: What should I do if someone with diabetes becomes unconscious?

### Answer

Call emergency services. Place the person in a safe position if you are trained to do so. Do not put sugar, rice, water, or tablets in the mouth of an unconscious person. Follow dispatcher instructions. If the person has a prescribed glucagon product, only a trained person should use it as labeled.

## Relationship to e-doc glucose

The diabetes form collects glucose in mmol/L. A submitted value such as 2.5 mmol/L is in a range often associated with hypoglycaemia risk in people who monitor glucose, especially with symptoms. A very high mmol/L value with severe symptoms is urgent. The model label does not replace emergency assessment.
