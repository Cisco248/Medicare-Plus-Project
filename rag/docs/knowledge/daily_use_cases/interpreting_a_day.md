# Interpreting One Day of Recorded Health Data

## Real-world use case

A person opens a daily health summary after a phone or wearable sync. The record may include steps, distance, active minutes, estimated calories, resting or average heart rate, sleep duration, home blood pressure, and self-checked glucose. The useful question is what that day shows in context, not what disease the numbers prove.

## How to read a single day

- Name each recorded value with its unit, time window, and source (clinic, home device, wearable estimate, or self-report).
- Treat missing fields as unknown. Do not fill them with zeros, averages, or “normal.”
- One day is a snapshot. Rest, travel, illness, weather, work, caregiving, device non-wear, and sport that a step counter misses can all change the record.
- Compare a day with the person’s recent pattern when that pattern is available. Do not compare one day with a marketing target as if the target were a diagnosis.
- Clinical measurements and documented diagnoses outrank wearable estimates when they disagree.

## What a daily summary can support

A complete day can support lifestyle education: movement relative to public-health activity guidance, sleep duration relative to adult sleep guidance, and whether home blood-pressure or glucose logs were recorded with useful context. It can also flag when emergency warning signs are described, in which case wearable totals are secondary.

## What a daily summary cannot do

Steps, calories, sleep scores, and wrist heart rate cannot diagnose diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, arrhythmia, or a sleep disorder. A single elevated home blood-pressure reading is not a hypertension diagnosis. A single glucose value is not a diabetes diagnosis. Estimated calories do not prove energy balance or diet quality.

## Safe language

Prefer “was recorded,” “is associated with,” and “may be worth discussing with a clinician.” Do not invent measurements, medicines, or symptoms. Do not recommend starting, stopping, or changing medicine.
