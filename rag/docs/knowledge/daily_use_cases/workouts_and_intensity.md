# Workouts, Active Minutes, and Intensity

## Real-world use case

A daily summary lists a walk, a gym session, or vendor “active minutes.” The user wants to know if the day “counts” toward official activity guidance. CDC explains that adults can meet the 150-minute weekly moderate-intensity goal in shorter bouts, such as 30 minutes on five days. Intensity is about effort, not about a brand’s badge.

## What counts

CDC’s “what counts” pages treat brisk walking as a common moderate example. A practical talk test: moderate activity raises breathing but still allows conversation; vigorous activity makes speaking more than a few words harder. Muscle-strengthening on two or more days is a separate recommendation. NHS adult activity pages give the same 150-minute / 75-minute structure for adults 19 to 64.

WHO’s physical-activity fact sheet matches this minute-based framing and encourages replacing sitting with movement of any intensity.

## Wearable intensity

Harvard Health’s wearable-workout explainer notes that watch heart-rate zones often use 220 minus age as a rough maximum. That estimate misses medicines, fitness, and individual variation. Active-minute totals may require a minimum heart-rate or step cadence set by the vendor, so they are not automatically equal to CDC moderate minutes.

Mayo Clinic’s exercise-intensity article describes perceived exertion and target-heart-rate ranges as tools, not as diagnostic tests.

## Safe interpretation

- Record activity type, duration, and whether intensity was self-rated, talk-tested, or device-estimated.
- A day with few active minutes can still include cycling or strength work the step algorithm missed.
- Chest pressure, faintness, or sudden neurological symptoms during exercise need emergency care.
- Do not prescribe a training plan for people with known heart disease, pregnancy complications, or recent inactivity beyond general public-health education.
